Activity I: Connecting with Nature

A compilation of our team's observations and reflections while spending intentional time with trees in our local communities.

John Benedict P. Supnet

The Observation: The tree I found was on campus, medium-sized, branches low enough to actually grab onto. The bark was rough and dry, the kind that tells you it has been standing there long before anyone thought to put a parking lot next to it. The leaves were not dense but they did the job, filtering out enough sunlight to make the ground underneath noticeably cooler than the open area a few meters away. That difference matters more than it sounds right now because it is genuinely, unreasonably hot outside. As a cyclist I feel this more than most. There is nothing quite like being stuck on an open road under full sun with no cover in sight, and then turning a corner to find a row of trees arching over the path. That shade is not just pleasant. It is a rescue. And what I noticed standing under this tree is that it was not just blocking light, the air underneath was actually cooler, because trees release moisture through their leaves and bring the surrounding temperature down. No electric fan does that.

The Reflection "Hugging it felt ridiculous for about five seconds and then it did not. When I climbed up and sat in the fork of the branches, something clicked. It just felt natural, like some part of my brain that rarely gets used finally had something to do. It reminded me of my grandparents' land, roughly two hectares of what I can only describe as a proper haven. Trees everywhere, fruits you could just reach up and grab, ground that stayed cool even at midday because the canopy above it never really let the sun win. I spent a lot of time there as a kid and I think that is where the instinct to climb came from, and probably why being around trees still feels less like an activity and more like going back to something familiar. Maybe that is just me. But I think the urge is older than any of us, a remnant of something our ancestors understood without needing to articulate it. Sitting up there on that campus tree, looking down at the parking lot and the motorcycles, I was reminded that trees are not decoration. They are infrastructure for being human, and we do not notice that until we are standing under one that is doing exactly what it has always done."

John Marcellin E. Tan

The Observation: The tree was old, crusty, thick, and was funny looking. Maybe this was because of how many times its trunks were cut. Cause maybe, this tree was here years before the school was even built. Who knows what this tree has witnessed and has gone through — how many buildings did this tree witness being built and destroyed; how many lovers have kissed under this tree; how many have cried under this tree? This tree might’ve even witnessed and felt all of the emotions a human has experienced. This tree is old and is still standing, no matter what’s happening to it and what’s happening beside it. This tree has gone through all of it. Even when surrounded by new buildings this tree still stands out—maybe weirdly, but it does. I wanna be like this tree. I wanna be old, crusty, and weird? But is resilient enough to go through it all.

The Reflection "It made me a bit sentimental, I guess? I don’t know why but that short time of me hugging a weird looking tree jokingly made me feel something HAHAHA, I mean not in the moment hugging the tree but after some time, just started to think about how I felt hugging the tree and it was weird how it made me sentimental. Maybe because unlike a tree, these moments, goofy times, and tomfoolery I share with my friends now might not be like the tree and see things through the end… :> Trees are important because besides literally giving us oxygen, they also serve as a reminder on how time never stops and that the best thing you can do is to accept it and try your utmost best to keep up with it."

Christian Jhon Ed J. Rosal

The Observation: The tree I chose stood quietly in a familiar corner of the center stage of Magsaysay Park, a very eerie surroundings, and yet approaching it with intention made me see it differently. It is an old tree — you can feel its age in the rough, uneven texture of its bark. A hookline is fastened to it, quietly supporting a nearby electric post, as if the tree has been carrying a quiet responsibility all along. What struck me most was how the roughness under my hands contrasted with the serenity it seemed to radiate. The atmosphere around it was still, dark, almost reflective — the kind of quiet that urges you to look deeper at things you usually walk past without noticing.

The Reflection "Hugging the tree made me a bit more reflective of my actions, oddly enough — something about the stillness of it loosened something in me. Even with a gloomy day and matching weather, my mood lifted a little. It reminded me of the phrase my BSCS 3 classmates often say about "touching grass" — that sometimes we all need to step away and reconnect with nature — something real and unhurried. Beyond what it did for me personally, trees are genuinely vital to the ecosystem. They provide shade, food, and shelter for lots of animals, and they make the world around them feel more alive. That old tree, hookline and all, is still giving."

John Paul L. Ullege

The Observation: Out near my neighbor's place sits a tree, one that ranges from medium to quite large. Thick bark covers its trunk, rough like old stone, while sturdy limbs stretch outward in every direction. Green leaves stay full and lively, casting cool shadow on the road beside it. A plain scene surrounds - sidewalk made of concrete runs close, a solid wall rises behind, and tiny plants peek through gaps nearby. Despite being stuck among built-up spaces, this tree manages to feel wild, breathing life into gray corners. Leaves once attached now lie scattered below, roots poking above soil, clear signs of years spent adjusting, holding ground. Time shaped it, just as much as weather and space.

The Reflection "At first, hugging the tree seemed strange, yet soon quietness settled in. Minutes passed, then peace arrived, slow but sure. Only then did I see trees differently - especially those ignored on sidewalks and streets. They offer shelter from sun, filter what we breathe, sustain creatures including us. Doing this brought clarity: their value runs deeper than scenery or silence. Wood comes from them, fruit grows on them, weather patterns depend on them too. In the end, one truth stood clear - Earth stays steady because trees hold part of its balance. "
