John Benedict P. Supnet

Certificate of Participation

Reflection Paper

Participating in the Malubago seedling planting activity along the Davao River was not something I had to be convinced to do. I initiated the connection between my group and the DMMA-led CWTS event through personal ties rooted in my family's long involvement in our barangay — my grandfather, a former president of the senior citizen and PWD association in Mandug, maintained relationships with several kagawads over the years. One of them happened to oversee the barangay's agriculture sector and was directly coordinating the planting event. That single connection made everything smoother than it had any right to be, cutting through the usual paperwork and permit requirements that would have delayed or derailed us entirely. I coordinated closely with the kagawad through multiple follow-ups, confirmed our slot as tag-alongs, and made sure we arrived at the barangay hall early enough to be properly introduced before the activity began. The planting itself felt natural to me in a way it might not have for others. Before college, I spent significant time doing farm work — digging, planting, and maintaining crops was just part of life growing up. On the day of the activity, the process was straightforward: dig the hole to the right depth, remove the black plastic casing from the seedling carefully, lower it in, cover it to the correct level, and stake a small stick beside it to mark the spot and protect it from being accidentally stepped on. My groupmates and I documented everything as we went, and despite one member arriving late and having to join another group, the overall energy was genuinely good. We were laughing, moving, working — it did not feel like an obligation. What actually stayed with me was something Councilor Al Ryan Alejandre said during his brief remarks before the planting began. He explained that the stretch of riverbank where we were working is notoriously prone to flooding, with soil erosion stripping the land during heavy rains. The Malubago, or Hibiscus tiliaceus, was specifically chosen because it holds soil in place with its root system while remaining resistant to prolonged water exposure — a plant doing quiet, unglamorous work to protect the people who live nearby. That framing shifted how I thought about what we were doing. It was not symbolic tree planting for the sake of a photo or a certificate. Each seedling planted along that bank is a small structural intervention against a recurring problem that affects real families in Mandug. I even took it upon myself to create and print the participation certificates afterward rather than wait for the barangay to process them, just to make sure my groupmates received documentation without unnecessary delay. Small actions, done properly, tend to matter more than grand gestures done halfway.

John Marcellin E. Tan

Reflection Paper

The modern world revolves on a myriad of problems making it slowly fall apart, in many ways we can’t ignore. Forests disappearing, seas littered with plastic, the air full of smog and pollution, and a climate that ever-changing and growing more unpredictable by the year. We keep building and expanding cities we can’t imagine, growth that is unprecedented by the past. And somewhere along the way, we forget that those spaces we occupy used to home trees and animals. They are the air we breathe, the food we come for, and the comfort we need. And don’t forget the roots that hold earth in after heavy rain. Without them, we’re more exposed than we realie–canopies that shield us from the sun before–and our quality of life suffers for it. That’s exactly why an event like tree planting matters more than it seems. There’s something different about doing something out of fun, and doing something out of need. Planting even one tree feels small, but when the crowd goes with you, now it feels more than just “feeling small”. You look around and see lots of people gathering for a common purpose, the people who showed up, who care, and who contribute to an air that’s lighter to breathe and an environment that’s healthier. And you realize that regardless of the position you are in, people can actually make a difference. That realization sticks. What I didn’t expect was how much the experience would feel building a community. Working alongside other people that you don’t even know working toward one shared goal creates this kind of easy, and comfortable energy, something that you’d grow into. People laughing, helping each other out, genuinely doing the work at hand. It wasn’t just about planting trees, it’s also being present with people and feeling connected to something deeper and bigger than yourself. That kind of thing where it’s making you more aware of the world around you, and more willing to protect and help it grow. Honestly, the day was great fun. The turnout was great, especially from the DMMA participants, and the site had this kind of calm, open, yet exciting atmosphere that made everything smooth. I actually had a lot of fun digging into the soil–there was something satisfying about it, like I was doing something for the world. I even wedged sticks around my sapling so no one would accidentally stomp on it, and did for other’s sapling too. It was hot, no question about that, but knowing that little sapling is out there growing made the sweat all so worth it. The biggest thing that I carried with me as I went home is that caring for the environment isn’t a one-time gesture. Planting a tree takes minutes, making sure it survives takes years, and that’s what makes me feel gloomy for a bit, the location was just so far from me to tend to it. Nevertheless, this activity served as a reminder that environmental stewardship is not just on the shoulders of organizations and the government. It is the responsibility of each and every individual– weall and in the small choices we make every day. I’ve become a lot more conscious of the resources I use, food scraps, excess water use, things we rely on a daily basis and the importance of preserving nature that provides these resources. In the end, when I think about why any of this matters at all, it comes back to the people who come after us. We’re planting trees that we may never get the chance to be comforted by its shadows, and there’s something beautiful beyond that. This activity reminded me that even the smallest actions we do, would echo and become bigger forward in time. It left me more grateful, more aware, and genuinely more motivated to improve the environment, as a naturalist. It drove me to keep showing up for these kinds of initiatives–because they don’t just help the earth become better, us as well.

Allan C. Tagle

Reflection Paper

Last Sunday, our class went to Mandug in Davao City for a tree planting activity as part of our subject on People and the Earth’s Environment. Even though it was a school requirement, the experience gave me a clearer understanding of why environmental responsibility matters in real life. Receiving a seedling and walking to the planting site made the activity feel personal and meaningful. It showed that each participant played a direct role in helping the environment. Being at the riverside also made me see why tree planting is crucial in places that are vulnerable to environmental changes like soil erosion and loss of vegetation. This activity helped me link our classroom discussions to real-world environmental action. Planting a tree was not just a task to finish but a reminder that small efforts, when combined, can lead to lasting environmental improvement. It also highlighted the importance of working together and sharing responsibility in tackling environmental issues. One of my key takeaways is that caring for the environment requires initiative and involvement, not just awareness. Simple actions like tree planting can help protect natural areas and promote sustainability. Overall, the experience deepened my appreciation for taking care of the environment and inspired me to be more mindful of how I can contribute in practical ways.
